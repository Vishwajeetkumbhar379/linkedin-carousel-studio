# Why do pigeons bob their heads? The 1-page guide

Read this in 3 minutes and you will know what a pigeon's head is really doing, how scientists proved it and which other birds do it.
Sources linked under every point, checked 3 Nov 2026 · @buildwithvish · November 2026

## 1. The short version
A walking pigeon isn't really bobbing its head. It locks its head still in space while its body walks forward underneath, then snaps the head forward and locks it again. The point is a steady view, not balance.

## 2. Hold, then thrust
High-speed film of walking pigeons shows two phases: one where the head is "locked" in space (moving backward relative to the forward-moving body), and one where it is thrust rapidly forward to a new position.
Source: [Frost, Journal of Experimental Biology 74, 187-195, 1978](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.74.1.187)

## 3. The 63% number
In Frost's film records the head was stationary approximately 63% of the time during walking, and each forward thrust moved at about 0.61 m/s. A later study measured the hold phase at 54% and 59% of the time in two data sets, so the exact share varies with the bird and how it walks.
Sources: [Frost 1978](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.74.1.187) · [Troje and Frost, Journal of Experimental Biology 203, 935-940, 2000](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.203.5.935)

## 4. The treadmill test
When pigeons walked on a treadmill, the hold-and-thrust bobbing disappeared. If the bird's walking speed matched the belt, its surroundings stopped sliding past and its head produced no bobbing at all. Frost concluded head-bobbing is "primarily a visual response rather than an equilibratory response" (one for balance).
Source: [Frost 1978](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.74.1.187)

## 5. The pigeon that fell over
By accident, the treadmill was once left on a very slow setting instead of off. It was too slow to make the bird walk, but the pigeon kept its head still anyway: its head was "slowly and progressively pushed forward until it eventually toppled over".
Source: [Frost 1978](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.74.1.187)

## 6. Why: a still picture on the retina
Researchers treat head-bobbing as an optokinetic response: holding the head still keeps the image on the retina stable, which helps the bird see. Many other animals get the same effect by flicking their eyes instead.
Sources: [Troje and Frost 2000](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.203.5.935) · [Theunissen and Troje, Frontiers in Neuroscience, 5 Oct 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2017.00551/full)

## 7. Who else does it
Head-bobbing shows up in at least 8 of the 27 orders of birds, including pigeons, doves, hens, starlings, pheasants, coots, quail and magpies. Next time you see a chicken walk, watch the head, not the body.
Source: [Frost 1978](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.74.1.187)
