ChatGPT is about to sign its EU texts. Invisibly.

Caption 

ChatGPT is about to sign its EU texts. Invisibly.

OpenAI announced it on 5 October. Over the coming weeks, eligible ChatGPT and Codex text made in the EU will carry a hidden statistical signal in its word choices.

What it can't do, according to OpenAI itself:

→ It can't tell who wrote or prompted the text
→ It can't tell how much a human changed it
→ It can't tell whether the text is true
→ No watermark doesn't prove a human wrote it
→ The detector isn't public. Only approved researchers and expert organisations can apply

OpenAI's own test numbers, at a 1% false-positive target: about 80% detection on 200-token passages of prose like psychology, about 95% on 400. Much lower for maths-like text.

What I'd do as a marketer this week:
→ Edit AI drafts for quality and facts, not to hide AI use
→ Check client and creator contracts for AI-disclosure clauses
→ Decide your disclosure rule before a client asks
→ Stop trusting "AI detector" screenshots as proof

Save this before your next client call.

Tue 27 Oct · Video · Real workflows and prompts

Before AI writes anything, make it brief you back.

Caption 

Before AI writes anything, make it brief you back.

Most weak AI drafts I see aren't a model problem. The AI misread the brief, filled the gaps with average guesses, and wrote confidently in the wrong direction.

Agencies solved this years ago with the brief-back. You hand over a brief, and before anyone writes, they tell you what they heard.

I now do the same with AI:
→ Paste the brief
→ Ask: what did you hear, what's missing, what are you assuming, how will we know it worked?
→ Correct the brief, not the draft
→ Then: "Write it, using only what we agreed."

Honest bit: when I first tried this, most of the gaps it found were mine. Vague goal, no audience, no line about what success looks like. The AI just made them visible.

It adds two minutes and saves a round of rewrites.

Thu 29 Oct · Video · Creator economy x AI

Use AI to rule creators out, not find them.

Caption 

Use AI to rule creators out, not to find them.

After 850+ creator deals, my view is simple: finding names is easy. Saying no to the wrong ones early is where the budget is saved.

So this is how I split the work:
→ Me: hard filters. Country, language, budget range.
→ AI: read the creator's recent captions and a few comment threads, and tell me what makes them a bad fit for this brief. Quote the evidence.
→ Me: check every flag myself. AI is fast, not always right.
→ AI: draft one opening line about a specific post for the creators who survive.
→ Me: rewrite that line so it sounds like a person.

What AI can't do: see private audience data, or prove comments are fake. Ask for audience screenshots and read the comments yourself.

Nobody reads your campaign report. They read the decisions.

Caption 

Nobody reads your campaign report. They read the decisions.

I've sat through a lot of campaign reviews on the creator side. The pattern is almost always the same: thirty charts, every metric given equal weight, and the meeting gets spent on slide two.

What gets read is page one, if page one says what to do next.

So I now run every report through three AI passes:
→ The so-what pass: 3 decisions (keep, change, stop), each with the number behind it
→ The sceptic pass: AI plays the finance lead and challenges each decision
→ The what-we-don't-know pass: what this data can't prove, and what to measure next time

One rule makes it safe: the spreadsheet does the maths, the AI only writes about numbers it's given.

The charts still go in the appendix. They just stop being the report.

Mon 02 Nov · Video · Contrarian takes

AI creators are cheap. Trust still isn't.

Caption 

AI creators are cheap. Trust still isn't.

AI avatar ads keep getting better. No contracts, no shipping product, no waiting for drafts, no reshoots. After 850+ creator deals, I understand the appeal more than most. Chasing approvals is real work.

But I think brands are mixing up two different things.

A creator isn't paid for a face reading a script. They're paid for an audience that believes them, built over years of posts, comments and the brands they turned down.

An avatar has none of that:
→ no comment section history
→ no record of saying no
→ no one who'd notice if it changed its mind

Where I'd use AI avatars: testing hooks and angles cheaply before spending on creators, explainer content, internal training.

Where I'd still pay a person: anything where the viewer has to believe the recommendation.

And if you use an avatar, label it clearly. Hiding it borrows trust you didn't earn.
