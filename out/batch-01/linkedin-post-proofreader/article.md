# LinkedIn retired its AI post writer and launched a proofreader instead

## LinkedIn swapped its AI writer for a proofreader

LinkedIn has rolled out a feature called **Post Proofreader** for eligible Premium members. It replaces the AI powered "post enhancement" option, the button that used to rewrite your draft for you, which LinkedIn removed the month before.

The difference sounds small. It isn't.

## What Post Proofreader actually does

According to LinkedIn, the tool "helps eligible Premium members improve post drafts before publishing." You see suggested changes before anything is applied, with inline change tracking, so you can check what changed and decide whether to keep it.

The options focus on three jobs:

- reviewing spelling, grammar and punctuation
- shortening a post while keeping its meaning
- improving clarity

What it no longer does is generate a post that didn't start with you. Social Media Today framed it well: the shift is about improving post language, not producing an idea that didn't originate from the person posting.

## Why LinkedIn made the change

The context is the "AI slop" backlash. LinkedIn added an option for members to report content they believe is low quality AI output. Less than three weeks after launch, that option had been used **more than one million times**. In September, LinkedIn extended reporting to comments as well.

So the platform is sending a fairly clear signal. Generated posts are a reputational risk for LinkedIn. Polished human posts are what it wants more of.

## What this means for marketers and founders

If you run a personal brand, a founder account or an employee advocacy programme, this matters in three ways:

1. **The tools are moving towards editing, not authoring.** Expect more features like this, and fewer "write it for me" buttons, on the platform itself.
2. **Readers are now actively flagging content.** A post that reads as generic can now get reported, not just ignored.
3. **Voice becomes the differentiator.** When everyone can produce clean text, the specific opinion, story or number is what earns attention.

I'll admit the limit here: LinkedIn hasn't said how reports affect reach, so I wouldn't claim it punishes AI writing. What we know is that members are reporting it in large numbers, and LinkedIn is redesigning its own tools around that.

## What I'd do

- **Write the first draft yourself.** Voice notes work. Bullet points work. It just has to be your thinking.
- **Use AI for the second pass only.** Ask for grammar fixes, a shorter version, or a clarity check. Keep your wording.
- **Read the tracked changes.** If a suggestion makes you sound like everyone else, reject it.
- **Brief your ghostwriters and agencies the same way.** Their job is to sharpen the founder's voice, not replace it.
- **Keep one specific detail in every post.** A real number, a real moment, a real opinion. That's the part no tool can supply.

From my own work across 850+ creator deals, the content that travelled was rarely the most polished. It was the most specific. LinkedIn seems to be betting on the same thing.
